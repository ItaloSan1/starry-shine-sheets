Before anyone gets in the basket

There is a toggle switch in the platform control box, wired with generic 16 GA white wire and Scotchlok taps, running down to the turntable. It is a hardware-store switch rated 10A 250VAC — not the sealed 12 V Genie 128200GT — with no boot, in a box we already know takes water.

Until you know what it switches, nobody rides this machine. It can command or interrupt something on the boom from the basket, and three of the circuits it could plausibly reach are safety circuits. Identifying it is section B, and it is the first real work of the day.

Five corrections since yesterday — read before you start

Every card in this plan was re-checked against the manuals by three independent reviewers. They caught these, and two of them change what you do:

1. The toggle is a six-terminal switch, not a three-terminal one. The body has two rows of three tabs — a double-pole switch with only one row wired. So B1 also has to probe the three bare tabs, and the "is it single or double throw" logic only holds for the wired row.

2. There is only one Scotchlok tap, and it is at the turntable. I told you there was one part-way down the boom as well. There is not — both your photos show the same splice, beside the tilt sensor carton. The white wire runs platform to turntable with one tap at the far end.

3. That tap is on an orange/black wire, with a red wire alongside, into a 2-pin black Deutsch plug. Orange/black on this machine is C64LS-OR/BK (circuit 64, power for operational switches) or C124SBS-OR/BK (the secondary boom angle operational signal). Both are on the stop-list. Open this one first.

4. Your ground control box is 237069GT (from SN 1712). 106512GT is SN 1711 and earlier — wrong part for this machine.

5. Solenoid coil range is 3.5–10 Ω, not the 3.5–5.5 quoted on task B5. Also now verified rather than guessed: Y74's return is VLVRTN6-BR from TCON J14-30.

Full detail: the complete verified plan — every connector, location, wire colour, expected value and its manual page — is in field-plan-2026-09-13.md in the repo. This page is the short field version for ticking off.

What I think happened here

Read together, the photos tell one story: parts were swapped, the calibration that every one of those parts requires was never run, and when the faults stayed, the repairs escalated into splices and bypasses.

01

The angle sensor was replaced and never calibrated

The bright blue hardware at the pivot is the 217246GT kit — the parts manual says the old 94980GT is no longer available and "machine calibration is required after installation." One of your live faults is literally primary boom angle sensor not calibrated. That is the fingerprint of a part fitted without the procedure.

02

A calibration was started and abandoned

TCON–SCON calibration inconsistent means the two modules' stored calibration data disagree. You get that from a partial or failed calibration attempt, not from a dead module.

03

Somebody found a ground problem

The added green wire to a splice at the pivot is a bonding jumper. Nobody runs one of those for fun — it means a previous tech suspected or measured a bad sensor ground. A high-resistance SNSR GND-BR produces exactly the crosscheck and 0 V faults on your display, and it is shared between the primary and secondary sensors, which is why both are complaining.

04

The toggle switch is a workaround for the failing membrane

Your membrane panel already throws "both axle buttons pressed." Genie's own answer to that is kit 237225GT, a toggle-switch lid, and the service manual carries a whole Toggle Switch Aftermarket Kit schematic for it. Someone improvised their own version instead of buying the kit — which is why it is a hardware-store switch on Scotchloks rather than a sealed one on Deutsch pins.

05

The TCON and SCON are probably fine — do not buy one yet

The engine runs, so the SCON is powered, grounded and on the CAN bus: loss of CAN drops P_9B, which is ignition and fuel, and the machine would die. All six chassis sensor codes cleared the moment the axles moved, so the TCON's feed, the DCON and the bus are all healthy. Dead modules do not fail this selectively. Replacing a SCON also forces a full machine calibration anyway, so it fixes nothing you are not already facing.

A. Stand down

Do first

Two minutes that keep tomorrow from becoming a bigger job.

Tag the toggle switch out and keep everyone out of the platform

Write "DO NOT OPERATE – unidentified circuit" on it. Nobody operates from the basket until section B is finished.

Photograph every splice before you touch it

Both Scotchloks at J114, the loose pin, the green bonding wire, the toggle's three terminals, and where the white wire enters and leaves each loom. You are about to take apart somebody else's undocumented repair — you need to be able to put it back.

B. Identify the toggle switch

Highest value

Battery disconnected for the resistance work. This is the one unknown that blocks everything else, including deciding whether the machine is safe to move again.

Ohm ALL SIX terminals in both lever positions

Tells you what kind of switch it is: which pole is common, whether it is single-throw or double, and whether it is momentary or maintained. Genie's 128200GT is SPDT momentary; if yours is maintained, it was meant to latch something on — which narrows the possibilities a lot.

Ring out each white conductor end to end

Buzz from each switch terminal to the far end. Note which terminal lands where. One will likely be a feed and one a return — find out which.

Open the Scotchlok at the turntable — host wire is orange/black

The tap tells you the circuit. Genie wire names carry their circuit number, and the colour confirms it — so a green/white host is circuit 109 sensor power, an orange/black host is circuit 64, and so on. Use the table below.

Check it against the stop-list — and stop if it hits one

If the toggle touches the foot switch, operational switch power, the tilt sensor, or either boom angle signal, it is defeating a safety function. That is not a repair to preserve — it comes out, and the machine does not work until it does. Send me what you find before you change anything.

Circuit numbers: SM pp.211–213 · SCON matrix: SM p.189
Best single guess: check the function enable valve Y74

This is the hypothesis that explains everything at once. Energising an axle solenoid does nothing unless Y74, the function enable valve, is also energised — it is what lets the lift pump supply pressure to all boom and steer/axle functions. Anyone who discovered that jumpering the axle valve moved nothing would need a way to hold Y74 on, and a toggle in the basket wired down to the turntable is exactly that way. It also explains why your direct-to-solenoid trick worked when it did.

Find it: behind the medium pressure filter. Ohm the coil — 3.5 to 10 Ω. Do not go by connector number: the schematic labels it J166 but the connector legend says J166 is the jib bellcrank sensor, so the manual contradicts itself here. The adjacent feed wire on the sheet reads V155PSE-OR/RD — an orange/red host wire under one of those Scotchloks would just about confirm it.

If that is what it is: the machine has had its master hydraulic interlock latched on from the platform. Every boom and steer function has had pressure available at all times, regardless of what the safety system wanted. It comes out.

C. The J114 boom angle circuit

The actual fault

Key on and E-stop out for the voltage checks; battery off for resistance. J114 pins: 1 and 6 are 5 V, 2 and 5 are sensor ground, 3 is the safety signal to the SCON, 4 is the operational signal to the TCON.

Unplug J114, measure 5 V on the harness side

Pin 1 to pin 2, then pin 6 to pin 5. Both should read 5.0 V. If either is missing, the problem is upstream of the sensor and the new sensor was never the fault.

SM pp.177–178 recovery action
With J114 still unplugged, check J154 and re-read the faults

The secondary sensor shares the same 5 V and the same ground. If the secondary's 5 V comes back and its "shorted / 0 V" code clears while the primary is unplugged, you have found the root cause — the primary splice job is dragging down the shared supply. This single test is worth more than any part you could order.

Ohm the Scotchlok loop — which two pins does it join?

If it joins pin 3 to pin 4, someone bridged the safety signal to the operational signal so the SCON and TCON would see one value instead of two. That is the crosscheck defeat, it must come out, and no calibration will ever pass with it in place.

Identify the loose pin's cavity number

Read the number moulded beside the empty cavity on the connector face, and buzz the terminal back to the loom to see which circuit it belongs to. An open pin 3 or pin 4 alone explains a crosscheck fault.

Measure the sensor ground back to the TCON

Battery off. From J114 pin 2 and pin 5 to TCON J12-25. You want under 1 Ω. Anything higher is your fault, and it explains why a previous tech added that green bonding wire instead of fixing the real conductor.

Confirm the 5 V LED is lit on the TCON board

The manual's own recovery action for every 0 V sensor fault. Thirty seconds, and it separates a module output problem from a harness problem.

SM pp.177–178
Trace the green bonding wire — what is it actually bridging?

You said it looks like it just joins two pieces of metal. Ohm each end to chassis and to J114 pin 2. If it is bridging a pivot joint, someone was compensating for a ground path through a greased pin — a real problem, badly fixed.

D. Survey while you are in there

Cheap information

Everything here is free and answers a question you will otherwise have to come back for.

Which joystick is the new one, and what does it control?

A replaced joystick needs its own calibration — it is step 2 of the calibration sequence, before any sensor. If it was swapped without that, it is one more uncalibrated part in the stack.

SM pp.30–33
Is the tilt sensor in that box fitted, or still spare?

There is a tilt sensor carton sitting at the turntable in your photo. Read the part number off it and check whether the sensor on the machine matches. If it was fitted, that is another part that demanded a calibration nobody ran.

Inspect the boom cable track for chafed conductors

This is where this harness wears. A broken conductor in the track is the most likely honest reason somebody started splicing in the first place — and if one is broken, others are on their way.

Read the software version off the LCD

It shows when you pull the E-stop out. 4.01 or higher means the 2-point calibrations and no digital level. Below 4.01 means 6-point procedures and you must have kit 58351. This decides what the calibration visit costs.

SM p.105
Re-read and photograph the full fault list

Capture it after all the unplugging, so we can see what moved. Faults that appear and disappear as you disconnect things are the ones that point at wiring.

F. The decision that ends this job

Book it

Nothing above makes the machine drive. Propel stays off until the crosscheck faults clear, and those clear only when the wiring is right and a calibration has been run.

Restore J114 to factory before anyone calibrates

Scotchloks out, proper Deutsch terminals in, every pin in its right cavity, the bonding wire replaced by a repaired ground conductor. A calibration run over improvised splices will either fail or store bad data — which is very likely how the "calibration inconsistent" fault got there in the first place.

Book the Genie-trained tech with room to extend the axles

The full calibration runs in a fixed order and the boom angle steps require the axles fully extended — you cannot do it on a narrow track. Make sure wherever it goes has firm, level ground and 13 ft of width, or the visit is wasted.

SM p.105 · steps 5–9
Book the annual inspection for after the repair

A CSA B354.4 annual inspection by a qualified person is required before this machine goes back in service after control-system work. Given what has been done to it, that inspection is the thing that protects you.
